What you just saw — and what is true.

Physics
Haze whitening the sky, and the hills fading before the rooftops do. Fine particles scatter light almost equally at all visible wavelengths, so they add white to the sky and wash out the blue that air molecules alone would give you. Contrast falls off exponentially with distance, which is why the farthest things vanish first.
Measured
Every visibility distance in this work is computed with the Koschmieder relation — the same equation used in air-quality monitoring — from a PM2.5 concentration you can set yourself.
Documented
Guidance that moves children indoors above certain pollution levels. Health agencies publish these thresholds, and schools act on them.
Light, not dust
Most of the stars this work takes away are lost to artificial light at night, not to particles. Aerosol haze contributes by scattering that light back down, but skyglow is the dominant cause. These are different problems and the artwork does not merge them.
Memory
The mother. The kite. The blue crayon. The small yellow shirt. Those are invented, and they belong to everyone who recognises them.

Remember When — an interactive artwork by Aanya Sharma

A text description, for anyone who cannot experience the interactive version.

Chapter one — the world came inside

A dark bedroom before sunrise. A clock ticks. A mother's silhouette crosses the room and takes hold of the curtains. You part them yourself, and the room fills with low orange light; dust turns over in the beam. You lift the window sash and the outside enters — birdsong, a breeze that moves the curtain, the leaves of a plant, the pages of a book, the hem of a bedsheet.

Chapter two — life happened outdoors

The window frame widens until it is no longer a frame, and the curtains lengthen into bedsheets on a washing line. You push between them; sunlight comes through the weave; your mother pegs another sheet further down the garden. Among the washing hangs a small yellow shirt that used to be yours. A corner of a sheet lifts free and folds itself into a paper kite. You fly it — pulling the line to climb, letting it out to drift — and as it climbs, the afternoon becomes evening and the evening becomes night. The kite becomes a star; the line you were holding becomes the line between two stars. You drag the whole sky and trace the shape you always knew: it is the shape of a kite. A meteor crosses; you can catch it. Two bright stars swell and become the two lenses of a pair of binoculars, and it is daylight again. Through them you find the far hills, the water tower, a poplar row, the rooftops, your school. The lens circle shrinks to a yellow crayon sun, and you are looking at a drawing you made: a sky filled corner to corner with hard blue crayon, gone over so many times the wax shines, out past the edges of the paper. Your mother kept it.

Chapter three — the change is almost invisible

The same garden, some years later. The sheets are still there, but grit settles on them. You can brush it off. It comes back. The same hill, the same kite — it climbs and then simply stops being visible, while the line stays in your hand. The same sky at night, and the shape you completed has stars missing from it. The same horizon, and the binoculars no longer help, because what is between you and the hills is not distance. The same drawing, its blue slowly bleaching towards the colour of the sky outside.

Chapter four — habits change before anyone names the cause

The washing comes indoors. Nobody says why.

Chapter five — recognition

The first bedroom, the same morning light, the curtains closed. You part them. You reach for the window latch, and something stops you: a reading of the air outside, 168 micrograms of PM2.5 per cubic metre, and advice to keep the window shut. This is the first time the work names air pollution.

The evidence

The memories themselves become the evidence. Soot from the windowsill is magnified until PM2.5 can be compared with a grain of sand, a human hair and a red blood cell. A slider sets the concentration of fine particles in the air, and the hills you searched for advance and retreat in response — the distance computed with the Koschmieder relation, visual range equals 3.912 divided by the extinction coefficient, the same equation used in air-quality monitoring. Two spheres of light show why molecules make a blue sky and larger particles make a white one. The blue crayon draws a graph of visibility against concentration. The constellation rearranges into the finding that 99 percent of the world's population breathes air exceeding WHO guideline levels, and separately into the finding that night skies are brightening about 9.6 percent a year — which is light pollution, not dust. A breath traces where particles of different sizes stop inside the airway, and why a child, breathing faster and taking in more air for their body weight, receives a larger dose. Sources: the World Health Organization, NASA Earth Observatory, the US Environmental Protection Agency, UNICEF, and Kyba and colleagues in Science, 2023.

The ending

Morning again, in the first bedroom. You part the curtains and everything from the whole work is out there at once, in one view, altered: the washing line, the rooftops, the hills where the hills used to be. You reach for the latch, and for a few seconds both worlds hold the same frame — your mother's hand over yours, the old blue behind the haze, clean sheets crossing the room, the kite above the roofs, stars where daylight should be. Then the memory withdraws. You may open the window. Nothing is repaired: a weak breeze comes in, the curtain barely moves, and the light is full of small things. Your drawing is propped beside the window, its sky still that deep, uneven blue. You may take the blue crayon and draw one small patch of it directly onto the glass, and through that patch the old world briefly shows. It stays where you put it.

My mother used to open the window before I was awake. I check the air before I do.
